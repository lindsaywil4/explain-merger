Section: Impacts

What lands on Houston if this goes through

The impacts are not abstract. They show up as minutes added to an ambulance run, trucks on a residential street, diesel exhaust over a schoolyard, and a rail yard growing toward the neighborhood next door.

Transportation: the street level

Start small. A blocked crossing closes a street. A stopped two-mile train closes several at once. In the East End, that translates into emergency response times of roughly 11 to 12 minutes, about double the 5 to 6 minute national average, because fire trucks and ambulances reroute around stopped trains. Commissioner Garcia's filing puts it plainly: first responders can lose up to 10 minutes at crossings when every second matters. Kids walking to school face the worst version of the choice, and FRA reports document children climbing through stopped trains to get to class.

Commissioner Adrian Garcia comment, STB filing 310429; East End Super Neighborhood briefing data, 2026; FRA Blocked Crossing Incident Reports, 2021–2026.

Transportation: the regional level

Zoom out and the costs compound. Rail crossing delays in Houston are projected to cost an estimated $2.6 billion over the next two decades in lost time, collisions, wasted fuel, and added emissions. About 40% of the region's freight moves through the East End's 16 square miles, so congestion here backs up the whole system. The merger routes more of the nation's freight through this same funnel while adding zero mainline capacity in Houston. The $2.1 billion capital plan concentrates Houston-area investment at Settegast Yard, mainly two additional 6,000-foot tracks and parking.

Commissioner Adrian Garcia comment (filing 310429); community briefing data; UP-NS Application Vol. II at 188 & 791 (STB filing 310577).
What does "no mainline investment" actually mean?

Mainline capacity is what lets trains keep moving: extra tracks, longer sidings, grade separations. Yard capacity lets a railroad store and sort more cars. UP's Houston plan buys yard capacity. So the city gets more trains and more trucks, and the same chokepoints those trains must squeeze through. That is the recipe that produced the 1996-98 meltdown.

UP-NS Application Vol. II at 791 (filing 310577); "What UP Says vs. What UP Filed," Not Another Houston.

Environmental hot spots and cancer clusters

The neighborhoods absorbing this traffic already carry a documented health burden. The Texas Department of State Health Services has identified cancer clusters in Houston's Greater Fifth Ward and Kashmere Gardens, areas that sit alongside Union Pacific's rail corridor and a former rail wood-preserving site contaminated with creosote. Settegast Yard sits in this same corridor of northeast Houston. Community filings and briefings have repeatedly connected the merger's added diesel truck and locomotive traffic to these existing cluster areas.

Texas DSHS cancer cluster assessments, Greater Fifth Ward / Kashmere Gardens (2019–2021); community briefings to Harris County and Texas House offices, Feb.–Mar. 2026.

An independent rail consultant told the Board directly that the merger's plan concentrates millions of truck movements into a handful of urban intermodal terminals, which "creates severe localized environmental impacts" at those terminals. The consultant's key point: because trucks are operated by UP's intermodal partners rather than the railroad itself, those impacts were excluded from the application's environmental analysis. The pollution is real. The paperwork just assigns it to someone else.

Atlantic Systems comment, Jan. 15, 2026, STB filing 310718, at 4, Docket FD 36873.

Houston is also a severe ozone nonattainment area, which is supposed to trigger environmental review at lower thresholds. Whether UP's traffic numbers cross those thresholds is contested. See the Discrepancies page for how the filed increments hover just below the trigger lines.

49 C.F.R. § 1105.7(e)(5); STB filing EI-34223.

Those trucks do not teleport to the freeway. They travel neighborhood arterials through northeast Houston and the East End to reach the yard. The public letters promised the merger would reduce highway congestion. The filings move freight off long-haul highways by adding short-haul trucks inside the city, concentrated in the same zip codes that already host the yards, the port traffic, and the cancer clusters.

STB filing EI-34157; Atlantic Systems comment (filing 310718); "What UP Says vs. What UP Filed," Not Another Houston.

The Settegast Yard expansion

To handle the growth, UP plans to expand Settegast: two additional 6,000-foot tracks plus added truck parking. Community briefings with Council District B and District H offices have flagged a related proposal affecting Kirkpatrick Road, where UP has discussed taking over or closing road access to fit the new tracks. Kirkpatrick provides access to the neighborhood depository, and nearby residents report UP had not briefed the community before the proposal surfaced. Council offices are pressing for answers.

UP-NS Application Vol. II at 791 (filing 310577); community briefing records with Houston City Council offices, Mar. 2026.
Who oversees whether any of this gets mitigated?

The STB does, through conditions on approval and through its environmental review. That is why the scope of the environmental review matters so much, and why community groups and officials are fighting to get the East End's crossings and the Settegast truck routes inside that scope rather than outside it. Cities can also negotiate. Chicago extracted roughly $400 million in mitigation from the CPKC merger. Houston advocates argue this deal's Houston footprint justifies far more.

49 C.F.R. Part 1105; community briefing records, 2026.
